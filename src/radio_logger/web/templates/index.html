What these numbers mean for this radio
Heard here
Callsigns decoded on the one frequency this receiver is tuned to. That count is what the radio heard. A high solar index does not override a quiet dial, and a quiet dial does not mean every other band is closed.
SFI
10.7 cm solar flux. It changes over days, not minutes. Higher flux tends to support higher HF, including 15 m, on daylight paths.
Sunspots
A slow count of the solar cycle, moving with SFI. More spots over months usually mean more good high-band days.
Kp
Planetary geomagnetic index for the last three hours, from 0 to 9. Around 4 and above, polar paths from Singapore toward Europe and North America often weaken. Japan is less exposed than those paths.
Ap
The day’s geomagnetic average. Same effect as Kp, read more slowly.
Solar wind
Speed of the wind arriving at Earth. A rise toward 500 km/s or more often comes ahead of a higher Kp.
Bz
North-south direction of the solar magnetic field. A negative, southward value can raise Kp. A positive value is the quieter sign.
Bt
Strength of that solar magnetic field. Strong Bt together with southward Bz is the pair that disturbs HF.
X-ray
Current flare class. A and B are ordinary. M and X, while Singapore is in daylight, can fade HF on the sunlit side of Earth for minutes to an hour.
R
NOAA radio-blackout scale from a flare. R1 or higher means sunlit HF can drop out. This station feels it when the sun is up.
S
Solar radiation storm. It hits paths over the poles more than Singapore itself, so Europe and North America can fade while local Asia still decodes.
G
Geomagnetic storm scale. G1 is already a disturbed day. Higher G makes the polar paths worse.
Protons, electrons, aurora
HamQSL’s extra disturbance readings. High protons and aurora go with the same polar-path trouble as S and G. They are not a count of stations.
HamQSL bands
Their global Good, Fair, or Poor for groups of bands, split into day and night. It is a general picture, not a measurement from this antenna.
